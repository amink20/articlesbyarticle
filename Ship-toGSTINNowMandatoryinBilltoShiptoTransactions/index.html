Ship-to GSTIN Now Mandatory in Bill-to/Ship-to Transactions: GSTN's New Compliance Mandate

30 July 2026· 9 min read· 📄 ArticlesByArticle
SA
Sachin Agrawal
Executive, Taxcraft Advisors LLP

In many transactions, the buyer who is billed and the party who actually receives the goods are not the same. Goods are invoiced to one person but physically delivered to another, on the instructions of the buyer. Until now, the identity of that actual recipient — the 'Ship-to' party — was not captured on the GST portal in a structured, mandatory manner.

GSTN has now closed this gap. Vide its FAQs on Bill-to/Ship-to Transactions, Export Scenarios and API Impact dated 1st July 2026, GSTN has mandated capture of Ship-to GSTIN wherever the Ship-to party is a registered person, with effect from 1st August 2026. This article examines the scope of this change, walks through practical scenarios including the treatment for contractors and job-workers, and sets out the API-level impact.

The Rule in One Line

Core RuleShip-to GSTIN must be entered wherever the actual recipient of the goods is a different registered person from the buyer. Where that recipient is unregistered, the value 'URP' must be entered instead. Where the recipient is the buyer itself, this field does not apply at all.

Reassuringly, this remains a backend compliance measure rather than a transparency measure between trading partners. Ship-to GSTIN will not appear on the printed e-Way Bill, will not be shown to taxpayers or transporters, and will not be returned through GET e-Way Bill APIs. It is captured solely for verification by authorised officers, and commercial confidentiality between the Bill-to and Ship-to parties is preserved.

The Four Transaction Types

GSTN's FAQs classify all movements into four categories, based on whether billing and delivery involve the same or different parties:

  • Regular — Billed and delivered to the same buyer; Ship-to GSTIN not applicable.
  • Bill-to/Ship-to — Billed to the buyer, but delivered to a third party on the buyer's instruction; Ship-to GSTIN mandatory if the third party is registered, else 'URP'.
  • Bill-from/Dispatch-from — Billed to the buyer, but dispatched from a third-party location to the buyer itself; Ship-to GSTIN not applicable since the buyer is already the Bill-to party.
  • Combination — Billed to the buyer, but dispatched from a third party and delivered to a fourth party; Ship-to GSTIN mandatory if the fourth party is registered, else 'URP'.

Practical Examples

1. Simple Bill-to/Ship-to

A Ltd. (Chennai) bills B Ltd. (Bengaluru), but ships directly to C Ltd. (Mysuru) on B Ltd.'s instruction. Here, Ship-to GSTIN is C Ltd.'s GSTIN, since C Ltd. is a distinct registered recipient.

2. Delivery to the buyer's own branch

A Ltd. bills B Ltd. and goods are delivered to B Ltd.'s own warehouse, which is an additional place of business of B Ltd. itself. Since the Bill-to and Ship-to are the same person, this is not a Bill-to/Ship-to transaction at all — it should be entered as a Regular transaction, with the delivery address recorded in the place-of-supply field.

3. Combination transaction

A Ltd. bills B Ltd. Goods are dispatched from C Ltd.'s godown and delivered to D Ltd. as instructed by B Ltd. Ship-to GSTIN is D Ltd.'s GSTIN, while C Ltd.'s details are recorded as the dispatch-from party.

4. Export movement

A Ltd. bills an overseas buyer, but goods physically move from A Ltd.'s factory to a port or ICD for export. Since no registered recipient exists at the port, 'URP' is entered as the Ship-to GSTIN, while the actual port or ICD address and PIN code are still recorded accurately. This treatment does not reclassify the transaction as a domestic supply to an unregistered person — the nature of the export continues to be governed by the shipping bill and other customs documentation.

5. Merchant exporter

A Ltd. bills a registered merchant exporter, and goods move directly from A Ltd. to a port for export on the exporter's instruction. Bill-to GSTIN is the merchant exporter's GSTIN, and Ship-to GSTIN is the registered GSTIN at the port location if one exists, or 'URP' otherwise.

6. Contractor procuring material for a construction site

A contractor executing work at a client's site places a purchase order with a vendor, who ships material directly to the site rather than to the contractor's office. The site carries no GST registration in its own name.

Practical AnswerThe Ship-to GSTIN to be entered is the GSTIN under which the construction site has been registered as an Additional Place of Business (APOB) — whether that APOB sits under the contractor's own registration or under the client's, whichever entity the site is legally declared under. Where the site has not yet been added as an APOB anywhere, 'URP' should be entered until it is.

7. Job-worker's premises

A principal manufacturer is billed by a vendor for raw material, but instructs the vendor to deliver it straight to its job-worker's factory for processing. Ship-to GSTIN is the job-worker's GSTIN, since the job-worker is a distinct registered person receiving the goods.

Why the Contractor Scenario Deserves Attention

This scenario trips up EPC contractors and works-contract vendors more than any other, because a project site feels like a mere location rather than a GST identity. GST law, however, does not recognise a site by name — it recognises it only through whichever registration, principal or contractor's own, has declared that address as a place of business. The practical fix is therefore administrative: every live project site needs to be traced back to the GSTIN it sits under before materials start moving, so that the correct value is available at the time of e-Way Bill or invoice generation.

Impact on e-Way Bill and e-Invoice APIs

The mandate is being enforced through hard validations across three flows — the standalone e-Way Bill API, the combined Generate IRN and e-Way Bill API, and the e-Way Bill by IRN API. A missing or mismatched Ship-to GSTIN will now block e-Way Bill or IRN generation outright, rather than merely triggering a warning. Validations also check that the Ship-to State Code matches the state embedded in the GSTIN, and that the Ship-to PIN code belongs to that state — so incomplete master data on delivery locations will surface as hard errors rather than being silently accepted.

Readiness Checklist

  • Map every Bill-to/Ship-to and Combination transaction — including project and construction sites — to the correct GSTIN or 'URP'.
  • For contractors and EPC vendors: confirm each site is properly added as an Additional Place of Business, with matching state and PIN code.
  • Update ERP and API integrations to handle the new mandatory field and associated validations.
  • Complete testing in the Sandbox environment before go-live, since this is a hard validation and not a soft warning.

Conclusion

The Ship-to GSTIN mandate is a targeted but consequential change. It does not alter tax liability or invoicing principles, but it does demand that businesses maintain accurate, GSTIN-linked master data for every location goods are delivered to — including third-party sites, job-workers' premises and export-linked locations. Taxpayers who map this data before 1st August 2026 will avoid disruption at the point of e-Way Bill and e-Invoice generation.
